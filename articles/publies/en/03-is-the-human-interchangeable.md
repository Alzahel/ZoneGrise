# Is the Human Interchangeable?

There is a question I keep coming back to whenever we talk about creating with AI.

Imagine two people.

Give them the same model, the same tools, the same references, the same amount of time, and the same general objective.

Do they produce essentially the same thing?

If the answer is yes, that does not necessarily mean they did nothing. But it does tell us something important: their personal presence probably had little influence on the result.

If, on the other hand, the two projects move in very different directions, then we need to understand where that difference comes from.

From what each person notices. What they reject. Which problems they consider important. How they interpret a constraint, choose between two solutions, or decide that a proposal that is perfectly acceptable is still not the right one.

For lack of a better term, this is what I call the **substitutability** test.

> **If I replace the person behind the tool with someone else, what actually changes?**

## Activity is not the same thing as agency

With AI, it is very easy to confuse the two.

Someone can spend hours writing prompts, rerunning generations, changing parameters, comparing variants, and correcting details. All of that takes time. But time spent still does not tell us which decisions actually gave the result its shape.

If I ask a model to find the subject, the angle, the style, the structure, and the content, then simply choose the proposal I like best, my presence can remain fairly weak even after fifty generations.

Another person with the same objective might follow almost the same path: ask for several versions, keep the most convincing one, and publish.

In that case, the tool did not merely execute decisions. It made a large share of them.

And if I can replace the user with almost anyone without deeply changing the trajectory of the project, it becomes difficult to say the result strongly depends on that particular person.

## Not every decision carries the same weight

Conversely, the same tool can produce projects that diverge quickly depending on who directs it.

One person will consider a proposal finished where another sees only a draft. One will remove an accident; another will decide that the accident is precisely what gives the result something. A solution that feels satisfactory to one person can become the starting point for an entirely different direction for someone else.

Those differences show that a result can depend on criteria that are not fully contained in the tool.

But not every decision carries the same weight.

A model can make thousands of small execution decisions without necessarily determining what the project is. Conversely, delegating only a few highly structural decisions can be enough to move a huge amount of control.

Choosing the subject. Defining the angle. Setting the structure. Deciding the style. Determining what should be kept, removed, or transformed. Knowing when the result finally matches what you were looking for.

The more those decisions come from the machine, the easier the user becomes to replace.

There are obviously uses where AI does almost everything that gives the result its concrete form. You ask for an image “in this style,” a track “with this mood,” a story “about this subject.” It then decides the composition, details, rhythm, wording, colors, transitions, and countless other choices.

If the result works, you keep it.

In cases like this, it is not enough to say the human had the general intention or pressed the button. The intention may be real while leaving almost the entire form of the result to the machine.

And if ten people with the same request receive ten different outputs mainly because of the model’s randomness, that does not make each person strongly non-substitutable.

Difference between outputs is not enough.

What matters is whether **the important differences come from the person’s decisions or simply from the system generating them**.

## The amount of assistance matters, but not by itself

A 2024 study on ChatGPT-assisted writing points in this direction. Researchers presented 602 participants with different scenarios in which a writer received more or less help producing a short novel, either from a human or from ChatGPT.

As assistance increased, the primary writer was perceived less as the author, creator, and person responsible for the text. When participants judged the writer, whether the assistance came from a human or an AI did not significantly change the ratings.

We should remain cautious: the study measures perceptions, not an objective definition of authorship. But it at least suggests that the question is not simply “AI or no AI?” What also seems to matter is **what the person still genuinely determined**.

## The test measures neither talent nor value

Being difficult to replace does not mean being good.

I can have an entirely personal way of making bad decisions. The result may be highly recognizable without becoming any better.

Substitutability therefore does not measure quality.

It is not enough to define who is or is not the author of a work either. A collective creation can depend deeply on several people at once. A creator can also choose to build their work around chance, rules, or constraints that deliberately give up part of their control.

And most importantly, the test is not a legal one. A highly personal contribution does not settle questions of rights, training data, consent, credit, or compensation.

## So what are we actually replacing?

AI can replace an execution task without automatically making the person behind it interchangeable.

But it can also gradually replace the choices that give a project its direction, its form, and above all its flavor.

There is probably no magical threshold between the two. What interests me is the movement: as structural decisions migrate toward the tool, the person becomes less necessary to the result; as the project depends on their criteria, their trade-offs, and what they transform, it becomes harder to replace them without profoundly changing what is produced.

If replacing the person barely changes the result, then perhaps we have to accept that the machine did more than help produce it. It also replaced some of the choices that would have made that result personal.

So the real question may not be how much the machine produced.

**It is how much the result still depends on the person directing it.**
