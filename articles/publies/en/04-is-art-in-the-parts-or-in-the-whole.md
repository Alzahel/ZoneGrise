# Is Art in the Parts or in the Whole?

There are works in which no single element, taken on its own, is particularly extraordinary.

A simple melody. A familiar image. A few sounds. A mechanic we have seen before. A sentence that would probably be unremarkable outside its context.

And yet, once brought together, those elements can produce something none of them contained on their own.

That may be one of the things I find most interesting about creation: **the whole can make something appear that did not exist in any of the parts.**

The arrival of AI makes that question even more interesting.

Because if producing some of the building blocks becomes easier, part of the creative effort can move elsewhere.

We can obviously use that gain to produce more: more images, more text, more music, more content.

But we can also use it differently.

To build something larger, deeper, more coherent, or simply impossible to make when every component required enormous amounts of time to produce.

Making the parts easier to produce does not necessarily condemn works to become simpler.

It could produce exactly the opposite.

## The whole can create something the parts do not have alone

Cinema offers an almost exaggerated example of this idea.

The Kuleshov effect describes how the same relatively neutral face can be interpreted differently depending on the images it is edited alongside.

The face has not changed.

What changes is what surrounds it.

And yet, we no longer see quite the same thing.

The idea obviously extends far beyond film editing.

A piece of music can sound gentle on its own and become unsettling when paired with certain images. A sentence can seem harmless, then become brutal because of the sentence before it. A color can seem dull in isolation and become intensely present inside another palette. A silence can be nothing, then become exactly what gives weight to everything that came before it.

Context does not merely package the components.

It acts on them.

Gestalt psychology has long shown how strongly our perception of a whole depends on the organization and relationships between its elements, rather than only on those elements considered independently.

For creation, the consequence seems important to me.

The creative decision does not necessarily live entirely **inside** each building block.

It can also live between them.

In their order. Their contrast. Their rhythm. What we repeat, what we remove, and what we choose to leave room to breathe.

**Composing is not only arranging elements. It is deciding what they become in contact with one another.**

## When the building blocks cost less, ambition can move

For a long time, a huge share of the energy required by a project was absorbed by making its components.

To create a game, you need to write dialogue, model environments, animate characters, produce sound, program behaviors, design levels.

To build a gigantic fictional universe, you need to write its stories, document its places, imagine its characters, maintain its timeline, and gradually make sure all of it remains coherent.

Every additional idea has a cost.

Every character, every environment, every narrative branch adds work.

That creates a kind of **complexity budget**.

Even with a great deal of talent and ambition, there comes a point where a project has to stop somewhere simply because everything it contains actually has to be made.

AI could move that limit.

Not make it disappear. But move it far enough that projects which once would have been absurd become conceivable.

And this is where the question interests me more than the simple productivity gain.

If I spend less energy producing each building block, I can choose to invest that energy elsewhere: in coherence, in the interactions between systems, in the number of situations the work can contain, in its ability to react, or in the depth of its world.

The gain is no longer only about doing the same thing faster.

It makes it possible to aim for a more ambitious whole.

## Bigger does not necessarily mean more filled

Video games are probably where I can most easily imagine this shift.

Today, to make a world feel alive, developers often have to anticipate an enormous number of things: dialogue, quests, reactions, routines, events, variations.

We can imagine another approach.

A world where characters do not simply have a few lines of dialogue, but a memory of what they have lived through. Characters with their own goals, relationships, and conflicts. Factions whose decisions genuinely change the world. An economy that reacts to what happens inside it.

Stories could appear not because a writer anticipated that exact quest, but because several systems happened to meet in an interesting way.

We would gradually move from:

**“We wrote an enormous number of possible situations.”**

to:

> **“We built a world capable of producing its own situations.”**

In a project like that, creative work does not disappear.

It changes level.

You have to decide which rules create interesting behavior. What a character can understand or forget. How far a system can evolve without destroying the coherence of the world. Which limits keep the world from becoming simply chaotic.

The creator no longer necessarily decides every story.

They increasingly design **the space in which stories can appear**.

And that can produce something other than a world that is simply larger.

A world that works differently.

## Vast universes carried by far fewer people

The same idea fascinates me in fictional universes.

Take something like *Warhammer*.

What makes it feel like a real universe is not one story, but accumulation: factions, characters, places, events, beliefs, conflicts, and thousands of years of history that eventually begin to echo one another.

Creating and then maintaining something at that scale normally requires a huge number of people and a huge amount of time.

We can imagine new tools eventually allowing much smaller teams to manipulate a universe whose amount of information is far beyond what they could personally keep in memory.

Not simply by automatically producing thousands of pages of lore. That would probably be the least interesting version.

But by helping creators think at a much larger scale.

Can this character be here? What does this region actually know about that event? What political consequences should this decision have twenty years later? Does this new faction contradict something established elsewhere? Which details should appear in its architecture, language, or traditions so that the whole remains coherent?

The tool could then become less a machine that invents the world instead of its authors and more an extension of their ability to **think that world**.

A small team could attempt something that previously would have required an enormous organization.

And I find that possibility much more interesting than simply writing three times faster.

## What if some works became impossible to make any other way?

There is another step beyond that.

When new technologies appear, we often begin by using them to recreate things we already know how to do.

Then, gradually, forms appear that would simply make no sense without them.

Cinema did not remain merely a more convenient way to show theater. Video games are not simply films where you can press buttons.

They eventually developed their own possibilities.

I see little reason to think AI would be different.

We can imagine works built on quantities of data no human could process alone. An installation that constantly evolves with what is happening in a city. Music that never exists in exactly the same form twice. A story that develops differently for every reader while still belonging to a shared universe. Fictional characters that continue to live while nobody is watching them.

In those cases, AI would no longer be used only to make the components of an existing art form faster.

**Generation, adaptation, or continuous evolution would become part of the medium itself.**

The artist may have built less a fixed result than a system capable of producing many different states.

Like a composer writing a score rather than every one of its performances.

Like a game designer creating rules whose every possible playthrough cannot be predicted.

Sometimes creation may lie precisely in building something you accept you will never control completely.

## The creator can become more of an architect

I do not think this means making the components yourself will stop having value.

Some art forms are deeply tied to gesture, performance, mastery of a material, or the particular way someone plays, draws, writes, or sculpts.

But those may not be the only ways to create.

As components become more accessible, the role of the creator can sometimes move toward that of an architect.

Someone who does not necessarily lay every stone themselves, but who needs to know what they are trying to build.

What should be large. What should remain empty. What should support what. Where something should break the symmetry. And above all, why all of it should hold together.

In a generative work, that role becomes even stranger.

It is no longer only about designing a work.

Sometimes it means designing **the way the work can change without ceasing to be itself**.

Which rules can vary? What has to remain fixed? How far can you let the system surprise its creator? And at what point does the work lose what made it itself?

Those are not less artistic problems simply because nobody personally made every one of their manifestations.

They are simply problems located at another level.

## The whole cannot be reduced to its parts

This may be where this way of creating becomes most interesting.

If part of the work shifts toward rules, relationships, and overall coherence, then looking at each component separately is no longer enough to understand the work.

Two projects can use very similar building blocks and produce something completely different depending on how they are assembled.

And sometimes, that is exactly where what we remember about the work is found.

## What becomes possible

What ultimately interests me is not so much that AI allows us to do more with less.

It is that it may move the boundary of what one person or a small team can reasonably attempt to build.

There will almost certainly be much more content.

But what I hope for most is work whose scale, complexity, or way of functioning would simply have been out of reach before.

And from that point of view, I find the period opening in front of us extremely exciting.

Not because everything produced with these tools will be interesting. Obviously it will not.

But because we may only be beginning to glimpse what it becomes possible to attempt.

Larger works. More alive. More reactive. Forms that do not really exist yet and that we describe poorly simply because we are not used to seeing them.

And if that happens, looking only at what AI produced inside the work may cause us to miss an essential part of what happened.

The question will also be **what it made possible**.
