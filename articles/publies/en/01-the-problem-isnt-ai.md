# The Problem Isn’t AI, It’s What We Ask It to Replace

When people talk about creating with AI today, I understand why so many have an almost visceral reaction.

You only have to open X, YouTube, Instagram, or sometimes simply Google to run into an absurd amount of mass-produced content. The same images, the same voices, the same phrasing, the same mistakes. Things that seem to exist only because they could be produced quickly, not because someone genuinely had something they wanted to express.

I’m not going to try to defend that.

I actually think it is probably one of the worst ways to use AI.

What bothers me is taking that use case — extremely visible precisely because it produces so much — and using it to define every form of AI-assisted creation.

Because the same tool can hide two almost opposite intentions.

There is the person who uses AI to remove as much of the human as possible from the process: less time, less thought, less involvement, more volume.

And then there is the person who uses it precisely because they want to make something real that they already carry within them.

That second use is the one that interests me.

## AI did not invent empty content

Producing large amounts of mediocre material because quantity eventually pays is nothing new.

Long before ChatGPT, we already had websites built for SEO, spam, opportunistic copies of trends, and all kinds of production whose main purpose was to occupy as much space as possible at the lowest possible cost.

As early as 2011, Google was publicly talking about *content farms* and trying to reduce their visibility in search results.

The economic logic is old: if each piece of content is cheap enough to make, a hundred average pieces can be more profitable than one excellent one.

What AI changes is the scale at which that logic can operate.

Writing, illustrating, translating, generating a voice, producing a thumbnail, expanding an idea into variants — until recently, these still required time, several skills, and sometimes several people. Some of those barriers have collapsed.

For someone whose goal is to produce as much as possible with as little intervention as possible, that is obviously a gift.

And the outcome is fairly predictable.

If no one needs to ask whether the image is actually good, whether the text expresses anything, whether the video adds anything, or whether the result matches a particular vision, there is no reason for that production to have much personality.

The problem, then, is not only that AI generated the content. The problem is that **the entire process is organized so that nobody has to care**.

That may be part of what people mean when they describe AI content as “soulless.”

We sometimes imagine that a mysterious property is missing — something a human hand would physically add to every word or pixel. I think the absence is somewhere else.

What is missing is someone who had a reason to prefer this version over another. Someone who wanted a specific emotion. Someone bothered by certain choices. Someone willing to start again because, even if the result is technically correct, it still is not what they had in mind.

What is missing is someone who cared.

## The same tool can be used for the exact opposite

This is where I struggle with the idea that using AI automatically means no longer really creating.

AI can be used to avoid thinking.

But it can also be used to think more.

You can tell it:

“Here is what I’m trying to do. Where does it stop working?”

“I’ve been circling this problem for two days. What am I missing?”

“I don’t know this field. What do I need to learn so I can make a good decision?”

“This scene isn’t producing the emotion I’m looking for. Help me understand why.”

“Give me several directions. I know the ones I found don’t work for me, but I still can’t articulate what I’m looking for.”

In those moments, I am not asking the machine to get rid of me. I am asking it to help me go further.

I like the image of a **flavor enhancer** for this kind of use: something that does not replace what was there to begin with, but helps bring it out, sharpen it, or push it further.

The vision does not necessarily come from AI. It may have been there for years, or it may still be vague and become clearer as the project develops. The tool can help challenge it, test it, expose its weaknesses, or simply get past a block caused by a skill you do not yet have.

For someone working alone, that difference is enormous.

Creating alone is difficult not only because there is a lot of work. It is difficult because there are so many decisions to make alone. You doubt yourself, circle a problem without knowing whether it comes from the idea or from the way you are executing it, sometimes have no idea what the next step is, and eventually look at the whole project wondering why you ever thought you could finish it.

Being able to talk with a tool that follows enough of the context, raises objections, explains an unfamiliar field, or simply helps put some order back into what you are trying to do can change a great deal.

Not because it is always right, or because every answer is good. It is not. It makes mistakes, invents things, oversimplifies, and sometimes offers generic or completely irrelevant solutions.

But it becomes possible to maintain a coherent, ongoing conversation around a project over time, where before you often had to either know the right person, already possess the skill yourself, or remain stuck.

Using it well means staying capable of saying no — of **keeping the final say**.

A proposal can be technically better and completely betray the original intention. A direction can be coherent, appealing, well argued, and still provoke absolutely nothing in the person creating.

At that point, the question is no longer which version is best, but which one is mine.

## A simple question: am I still necessary?

I do not want to defend the opposite idea either: that anything involving AI automatically becomes “my work” simply because I wrote a few instructions.

If I ask a model to find the subject, choose the angle, produce the content, decide which version is best, and prepare the final result, my presence gradually becomes hard to distinguish from anyone else’s.

And that may be where a much more useful question appears than “Was AI used?”:

> **If I replace the person behind the tool with someone else, do I get essentially the same thing?**

If the answer is yes, their personal contribution is probably small.

But the more the result depends on what a person likes, refuses, understands, pursues, transforms, and tries to express, the less interchangeable that person becomes.

I think this idea of **substitutability** deserves an important place in the debate around AI-assisted creation. It does not answer everything, and it does not magically draw the line between “real artist” and “fake artist.” But it forces us to look at what the person actually brings to the process instead of stopping at the list of tools they used.

The question of where authorship begins, legally or artistically, deserves its own article. For now, this test is enough for me: if removing a person from the process profoundly changes the work, it is difficult to claim they put nothing of themselves into it.

## So what are we really judging?

AI is a tool. An extremely powerful one, and precisely for that reason, saying “it’s just a tool” is not enough either.

It can replace a tedious task, a skill we do not yet have, or an interlocutor we are missing. But it can also gradually replace judgment, decisions, and eventually almost all meaningful involvement from the person using it.

Those are very different uses.

So I am not trying to defend “AI art” as a single block, or pretend that every use is equivalent. I want to defend a simpler idea: **the presence of AI alone still does not tell us where creativity is — and certainly does not prove its absence.**

To know that, we have to look at what the person was trying to do, which decisions they kept, which ones they delegated, what they rejected, and why the final result exists in precisely this form.

Industrial content is especially visible today because its entire reason for existing is to be produced in quantity. But it would be unfortunate if, after seeing so many people use AI to produce without having much to say, we ended up treating the tool as illegitimate for those who genuinely had something to express.

Maybe the problem is not AI.

The problem is what we choose to ask it to replace.

And that is the boundary I want to explore in *Zone Grise*.
