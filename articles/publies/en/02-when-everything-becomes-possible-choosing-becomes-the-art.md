# When Everything Becomes Possible, Choosing Becomes the Art

A few years ago, with many creative problems, the difficulty was obvious: managing to produce what you had in mind.

Write the scene properly. Draw the character. Find the melody. Program the mechanic. Get the shot. Today, the problem is reversing.

I can ask for ten versions of a sentence. Twenty directions for an image. Several structures for a scene. Variations on a concept, an interface, a piece of code, or a character.

And sometimes the hardest part is no longer getting an answer. It is knowing which one deserves to remain.

We talk constantly about what AI can produce. Much less about what it forces us to choose.

## Producing a lot does not mean knowing what to keep

There is a fairly intuitive idea behind generative tools: the more possibilities I have, the more likely I am to find a good one.

That is probably true up to a point.

But having twenty proposals does not automatically give me the ability to recognize the best one. And “the best” rarely means much in a creative project anyway.

The most elegant sentence is not necessarily the one the character should say. The most spectacular image can be completely wrong for the world. The catchiest melody can destroy the emotion the piece was trying to build. An asset can be beautiful in isolation and bad once placed in a game.

I find that last case especially interesting, because it shows that taste is not only about judging elements separately. It is also about judging how they live together.

An excellent piece of music can make a scene worse. A less brilliant sentence can be exactly what the text needs. A fairly ordinary element on its own can become right because it finds its place in a coherent whole.

**In a work, the best element is not always the one that makes the whole better.**

That is a form of composition: not only producing or selecting building blocks, but understanding what they do to one another.

I will come back to this, because the question deserves an article of its own. But it matters here: when individual elements become easy to produce, knowing which ones can genuinely belong to the same project carries more weight.

Research on creativity has long distinguished idea generation from idea evaluation. Producing possibilities and knowing which ones are worth pursuing are not exactly the same skill.

AI can therefore make the amount of produced content explode without answering the next question:

**what am I actually looking for?**

It may even make that question much harder to avoid.

## Taste may appear most clearly in what we refuse

For me, taste in a project also means sensing that a good proposal simply does not belong there.

It means understanding that a line is too clever for this character, that a shot draws attention to the wrong place, that an interface that is objectively more modern no longer feels like the game, or that an idea that makes perfect sense on paper removes exactly the strangeness you wanted to preserve.

Taste is not only knowing what you like. **It is knowing what has no place here.**

And the faster a tool can offer plausible solutions, the more important that ability becomes.

Precisely because AI is very good at proposing something that works, “it works” is often not enough.

It has to work **for this specific thing**: this scene, this character, this rhythm, this direction, this intention. And sometimes what looks objectively cleaner, more spectacular, or better constructed is exactly what needs to be rejected.

The creator may therefore be visible not only in what they add, but also in everything they refuse to let in.

## But choosing is not enough

If I generate two hundred images from a vague instruction and select the one I find prettiest, I am not going to pretend that this act of selection suddenly equals the work of an illustrator who constructed every part of an image.

That would be too easy, and it is not what I am trying to defend.

A 2024 study on AI-assisted poetry writing found that participants who were placed mostly in the role of editing machine-generated text produced poems judged less creative than those written without AI. That gap disappeared when the tool placed the person in more of a co-creative process.

The distinction seems important to me. There is a difference between receiving something nearly finished, deciding whether to keep it, and repeating until an output looks good enough — and entering a loop in which every result genuinely changes the next direction.

In the second case, I can start from a proposal that does not work, understand through it what bothers me, change my intention, keep one detail, reject several others, combine elements, go back, or decide the entire direction was wrong. The final result then depends less and less on the model’s first answer and more and more on all the decisions that followed.

That, to me, is where choice becomes genuinely interesting. Not when it simply consists of picking a winner from a set of outputs.

But when it **changes the trajectory of the project**.

## Sometimes you also have to reject every proposal

There is another problem: having an enormous number of choices does not necessarily mean having an enormous amount of difference.

Research on AI-assisted creativity has found something fairly paradoxical: people can individually produce better results with generative assistance while collectively producing things that are more similar to one another.

That matters, because it means the right choice is not always hidden somewhere in the list. Sometimes the real work is noticing that every proposal is taking you toward the same place.

And stepping out of the corridor.

In other words, taste is not only the ability to recognize a good answer. It is also the ability to recognize that the space of answers being proposed is itself wrong for what you are trying to make.

## The new luxury may be having a criterion

I do not think execution will become irrelevant.

In many disciplines, learning how to make something changes how you see. Technical constraint creates discoveries. The gesture itself can be at the heart of the work. And that question deserves its own article.

But if some forms of execution become far less costly, their scarcity inevitably decreases.

Getting an impressive image may become ordinary. Technically correct prose too. Pleasant music. A working prototype. A clean animation.

What does not automatically become ordinary with them is knowing **why this version should exist rather than another**.

Perhaps AI does not make the creative problem disappear. It moves part of the bottleneck.

For a long time, the question was often: “Am I capable of producing this?”

Increasingly, it may become:

**“Now that I can produce all of this, what do I decide to keep?”**

As producing becomes easier, choosing becomes more important.

And when everything becomes possible, choosing may become an increasingly visible part of the art.
